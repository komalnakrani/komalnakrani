## MLE-CH-16-S01 — Decision and Bench Setup

### Bench Setup

BL-14 established an exact candidate and recovery identity. This chapter asks a different question: under a named synthetic demand and concurrency fixture, what serving envelope was actually measured, where did it fail or saturate, and what degraded or rollback behavior was tested? The incoming state is RELEASABLE; BL-15 may move it to OPERABLE only as a bounded technical conclusion. A green integrity packet does not predict latency, error behavior, resource pressure, or safe exposure.

The Bench Sheet fixes request shape, demand schedule, concurrency, timing boundaries, response classification, resource observations, and candidate and previous-good identifiers. Its decision is not “meet a universal SLO.” Its decision is to report the observed envelope honestly: latency distribution, errors, resource values, saturation point, and tested degraded or rollback route. The **state and dossier delta** is `RELEASABLE → OPERABLE` through BL-15. The **next evidence** is an observation contract, not a promise of outcome quality.

The MLE measures workload behavior and preserves the evidence. Platform and SRE owners retain fleet capacity, incident, shared-controller, and SLO decisions; product and domain owners retain effect and exposure decisions. A workload test may expose an abort condition, but it cannot self-authorize traffic allocation or turn one synthetic run into a capacity commitment. When a required owner or input is absent, the record holds and routes it outward.

Before timing anything, make the request boundary explicit. Does the clock start at acceptance, after queueing, at model invocation, or at response serialization? Which requests are excluded, and why? Is warm-up a named phase or silently removed? These details determine whether two runs describe the same envelope. They are more useful than a visually smooth chart because they let a reviewer reconstruct the measurement and recognize when a changed runtime, dependency, interface, or demand fixture invalidates it.

The traceability assignments are architecture `MLE-CLM-003`, `MLE-CLM-013`, `MLE-CLM-016`, and `MLE-CLM-018`; boundaries `BND-09`, `BND-10`, `BND-11`, and `BND-12`; scenarios `SCN-02`, `SCN-07`, and `SCN-09`; domain `PD-10`. The architecture set makes the complete workload, measured operating behavior, representative evidence, and recovery continuity auditable. The boundaries retain traffic, fleet, shared-platform, and exposure authority; the scenarios stress managed, edge, and change/recovery conditions; the domain names serving and observation evidence. This mapping does not duplicate the primary lessons.

## MLE-CH-16-S02 — Procedure: Describe the Envelope, Not a Slogan

**Primary teaching MLE-BCLM-046.** A serving claim needs a latency distribution, including decision-relevant tail percentiles under representative demand. A mean hides the slow requests that can dominate user experience when work fans out, utilization rises, or scale changes. Record sample definition, arrival pattern, concurrency, warm-up treatment, percentile method, successful and failed request counts, and the timing boundary. A p50 without p95, p99, error class, or fixture identity is a partial observation, not a decision envelope. MLE-BSRC-007 and MLE-BSRC-008 support production-readiness and service-performance reasoning, but neither supplies a universal latency target.

The lesson is not to elevate one percentile into doctrine. A tail value matters when it changes a stated decision; a workload with a strict user interaction may inspect a different part of the distribution than a bounded batch service. The conclusion must say which demand was used and which behavior was not sampled. Hardware, model server, and absolute threshold values are fixture fields with currentness dates, not transferable claims.

**Primary teaching MLE-BCLM-047.** A serving-envelope record binds named demand and concurrency to latency distribution, request errors, resource observations, observed saturation or failure point, and tested degraded or rollback behavior. Link those measurements to the BL-14 candidate and previous-good identity. An error spike without request classification does not explain whether the service, input, dependency, timeout, or refusal path failed. A resource chart without its sampling window does not prove steady state. A saturation observation without a named pressure point does not provide a repair boundary.

Its canonical support edges are MLE-BSRC-007 for readiness tests, MLE-BSRC-008 for tail behavior, MLE-BSRC-016 for deployment mechanics, and MLE-BSRC-040 for staged exposure. Their roles remain separate; none supplies the workload’s threshold or fleet authority.

The record can state, for example, that a synthetic fixture reached an observed concurrency where tail latency widened, a timeout class appeared, and the tested route restored the named previous-good package. It cannot state that a fleet is adequately sized or that a controller owns capacity. Kubernetes-related examples in MLE-BSRC-016 illustrate rollout state; they do not establish model correctness, a workload SLO, or platform authority. MLE-BSRC-039 and MLE-BSRC-040 supply bounded incident and operational context, not assurance by association.

Good envelopes also retain negative space. Record the demand patterns not exercised, the resource dimensions not collected, the regions or devices not represented, and the failure classes that could not be induced safely in the fixture. A team can then decide whether that absence requires a new test, a different owner, or a limitation at release. Omitting these gaps turns a bounded run into an accidental universal claim. Stating them preserves the value of the measured result while refusing to stretch it.

**Primary teaching MLE-BCLM-048.** Staged exposure compares a time-bounded candidate population with a control through attributable signals and absolute operating limits. The record names bake period, population scope, comparison signal, abort trigger, rollback route, degraded mode, and owner. A globally aggregated dashboard can conceal a failing canary, especially if the control and candidate populations have different request mix. The comparison should be framed as evidence for an exposure decision, not as a replacement for prior qualification or formal release authority.

Its canonical edges are MLE-BSRC-039 for bounded failure/control context and MLE-BSRC-040 for canary mechanics; neither converts a constructed comparison into a real outcome or authorization.

The limitations must be repeated rather than implied. No universal percentile, latency target, production performance, capacity guarantee, or business outcome follows from a synthetic fixture. A tested rollback might restore a deployment template while data, features, credentials, consumers, or runtime state need separate evidence. The chapter’s authority route preserves that distinction: platform operators decide fleet mechanics; evaluation, domain, and product owners decide the meaning of observed signals.

To read an envelope well, separate conditions deliberately controlled from those merely observed. Controlled inputs include request body, arrival schedule, concurrency, candidate digest, and previous-good digest. Observed fields include clock measurements, response class, and resource sample. The distinction stops a reader from mistaking a fixture’s configured concurrency for achieved capacity. It also reveals whether a changed request shape, dependency, server runtime, or routing layer means two envelope records should not be compared directly.

An abort rule needs the same discipline. It names the signal, scope, time window, route, authority, and required evidence after the route is taken. “Abort on bad metrics” is not usable because it names neither metric, population, nor owner. A record saying that a candidate timeout class appeared under a declared fixture, a comparison was preserved, a degraded route was entered, and fleet action was routed to operations can be inspected. It still does not decide real traffic policy.

## MLE-CH-16-S04 — Worked Trace: A Bounded Exposure Comparison

CASE-12 is a PUBLIC REPORTED CASE with a bounded issuer removal record. It illustrates why “we took one action” is not a full system conclusion. It does not establish that a canary was safe, that all consumers were cleaned, or that the event was an ML deployment. Keep its limitation beside the trace.

Keep its truth record separated: **reported facts** are the SEC’s deployment/control findings, automated messages, and issuer-stated software removal; **attributed outcomes** remain only those of the SEC and issuer, including the settled action; **allowed inference** is that serving evidence needs hard limits, attributable signals, owners, and bounded follow-through; **forbidden inference** is an ML failure rate, trading threshold, regulatory conclusion, safe canary, full cleanup, or terminal proof; **limitations** are non-ML trading software, no-admit/no-deny settlement, size-limited filing extraction, and no public full path audit; **transfer rule** replaces order/router details with the port’s actual workload paths while retaining fleet, compliance, risk, product, and domain authority externally.

In the trace, the candidate and control do not need identical raw counts to be compared. They need an attributable comparison rule and a declared reason why the populations are comparable. If a request class is absent from the control, the record says so. If a segment is too small for a decision, the record says so. If an abort is reached, it records whether the exercised route returned the workload to its named previous-good identity. This detail is what makes a staged exposure an inspectable experiment rather than a percentage written beside a chart.

## MLE-CH-16-S05 — Failure Labs

`MLE-CH-16-LAB-01` runs a fixed positive envelope fixture. It reports distributional latency, request classes, resource observations, a named pressure point, and the BL-14 and previous-good identities. Remove the tail distribution while leaving the mean. The deterministic result is HOLD because the decision-relevant tail is unobserved; the record must not substitute a dashboard headline.

`MLE-CH-16-LAB-02` injects a failing canary hidden inside aggregate traffic, a missing rollback identity, and a degraded-mode route with no owner. Each produces a named diagnostic and owner route. The aggregate-only mutation fails because attributable candidate/control evidence is absent. The rollback mutation fails because recovery cannot be located. The ownerless degraded-mode mutation fails because the MLE cannot declare platform action. These labs are offline and synthetic-deterministic: no provider, traffic system, credential, model, or real service is changed.

`FIX-MLE-CH-16-1` is the closed representative-trace input; `FIX-MLE-CH-16-2` is the closed mutation input. Each accepts the named candidate, BL-14 recovery identity, demand schedule, request classes, resource samples, and owner routes only. Legal dispositions are PASS, HOLD, REJECT, or REOPEN. Acceptance requires attributable tails, pressure evidence, degraded behavior, limitation, owner, and next route. Prohibited effects are network, shell, provider, credential, traffic, model, production, and authority mutation.

The labs demonstrate a useful discipline: distinguish “not measured,” “measured and outside bound,” and “measured but owned by another decision.” They preserve failure history and allocate a new candidate only when bytes or decision basis change. They do not turn a successful fixture into an approval.

The deterministic design also makes comparison reproducible. Re-run the same fixture and the evidence shape, decision fields, and failure disposition should recur; change the fixture and bind its new identity rather than merging results. This does not promise performance reproducibility on a real fleet. It teaches a narrower habit: evidence records must reveal which observations belong together and which do not. That habit survives a change of server, accelerator, vendor, or deployment controller.

## MLE-CH-16-S06 — Five-Port Transfer

`PORT-MANAGED`, `PORT-CLASSICAL`, `PORT-DEEP`, `PORT-EDGE`, and `PORT-SHARED` all use the same decision and evidence shape: named demand, latency distribution, errors, resources, observed pressure, degraded or rollback route, limitation, and owner. `PORT-MANAGED` needs exportable provider observations; opaque counters remain a limitation. `PORT-CLASSICAL` binds preprocessing and estimator execution. `PORT-DEEP` binds checkpoint, runtime, and accelerator context. `PORT-EDGE` adds constrained devices and delayed feedback. `PORT-SHARED` separates tenant evidence from fleet SLOs.

Mechanically, managed evidence fails on unavailable exports and routes to provider/platform authority; classical evidence fails on feature-order or preprocessing mismatch and routes to data/evaluation; deep evidence fails on checkpoint, preprocessor, or hardware-context identity and routes to research/platform; edge evidence fails on sensor shift, resource pressure, or stale package and routes to hardware/operations; shared evidence fails on tenant collision, upgrade, or fleet pressure and routes to platform/SRE/security. The limitations differ—provider opacity, simple-model blind spots, scale, fictional edge coverage, and workload-versus-fleet scope—but every row returns the same bounded envelope result.

Equal transfer means the port may change mechanics but not proof burden. A small estimator does not waive tail reporting. A large model does not gain status because its stack is complex. An edge device does not turn intermittent observation into an invented result. A managed provider does not make undocumented limits acceptable. The port result is a bounded envelope, never automatic promotion.

When a port cannot produce one requested field, do not replace the field with a claim of equivalence. Record the unavailable export, opaque counter, unrepresentative demand, or untested rollback behavior as a limitation, name the mechanism owner, and decide whether the remaining evidence is sufficient for the narrower state. The result may be HOLD. That is useful: it prevents an apparently convenient implementation path from receiving a quieter standard than the other four ports.

## MLE-CH-16-S08 — Durable Handoff

BL-15 carries the exact candidate and recovery identity, fixed demand fixture, latency and error observations, resource window, saturation or failure evidence, staged-exposure comparison, degraded route, limitation, source notes, currentness triggers, and owner routes. It hands an OPERABLE workload to observation without declaring outcome truth. A changed runtime, dependency, interface, serving envelope, permitted use, or authority returns to the applicable earlier gate. The chapter stops at the private handoff boundary and creates no deployment, image, public publication, or operational action.

Before accepting the handoff, ask whether a new reader could recreate the envelope without asking the original author what a number meant. They should be able to locate the candidate, control, demand schedule, timing boundary, response classes, resource window, pressure observation, and recovery route. They should also see what was not tested. That is the distinction between an evidence packet and a performance anecdote. It is especially useful when a later runtime upgrade appears to make a result better: the new measurement may be valuable, but it belongs to a changed envelope and must not silently overwrite the older one.

The final operational question is proportionality. The record needs enough detail to support its stated decision, not a theatrical claim of total capacity knowledge. If an owner asks whether the fixture supports a broader launch, the answer may be that the fixture supports only the recorded envelope and a new decision requires more evidence. This keeps the chapter squarely within its MLE responsibility: measure and communicate workload behavior while retaining the owners of traffic, fleet, product, and risk decisions.

One final consistency check compares the envelope to BL-14. The candidate identity, declared consumer, dependency conditions, and previous-good package in both records must agree. A timing run performed against an unbound image, an unspecified model revision, or a consumer path absent from BL-13 is useful diagnostic material, but it cannot be promoted into BL-15. This cross-check is intentionally mechanical: it links two pieces of evidence without asking the serving test to decide whether the workload should be released. If the identity does not resolve, the test is held and the packet returns to its owning integrity or compatibility gate.

This closes the measurement loop: the claim remains fixture-specific, identity-bound, limited, and routed to the owners who can decide exposure beyond the tested envelope.
